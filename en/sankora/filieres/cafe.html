    Coffee

    Coffee, a smallholder crop

    According to the International Coffee Organization (ICO), coffee is the main source of income for more than ten million households in 25 African countries. It brings vital foreign currency to several economies on the continent.

    Africa accounted for about 11.6% of world coffee exports in 2024/25, driven by Uganda and Ethiopia. Producing countries are aiming for a larger share by 2030.

    In Côte d'Ivoire, robusta coffee was long a flagship crop alongside cocoa. Output has fallen sharply since the 1980s and its revival depends on renewing coffee farms.

    > 10 millionAfrican households depend on coffee (ICO, 2015)
    ≈ 11.6%of world exports come from Africa (ICO, 2024/25)
    ≈ 72,000 tof coffee produced in Côte d'Ivoire in 2024
    Where it is grown in Africa

    Africa's main coffee countries

    East Africa now dominates production. West and Central Africa mainly grow robusta.

    1. EthiopiaAfrica's largest producer, birthplace of arabica, nearly 6 million coffee farmers.
    2. UgandaAfrica's leading exporter in 2024/25, mostly robusta.
    3. TanzaniaAfrica's third exporter, arabica and robusta.
    4. Côte d'IvoireMajor historical robusta producer, now rebuilding output.
    5. KenyaSpecialty arabicas sought after by roasters.
    6. CameroonRobusta and arabica, output declining since the 2000s.
    The value chain's challenges

    Challenges facing the sector

    01

    Low yields

    The ICO reported average yields of about 400 kg per hectare in Africa, with very little fertiliser use. Old varieties are vulnerable to leaf rust and the berry borer.

    02

    Ageing farms and farmers

    Many coffee trees are past their productive age. Young people are turning away from a crop seen as poorly paid.

    03

    Prices and income

    World prices are volatile and labour makes up most production costs. Transparent purchases and payments build farmers' trust.

    04

    Child labour

    Ivorian coffee, like cocoa, appears on the U.S. Department of Labor list of goods produced with child labour. Buyers expect monitoring measures.

    05

    Tracing small, scattered volumes

    Coffee is collected from many smallholders. Linking each lot to geolocated plots requires rigorous organisation.

    What markets require

    Coffee is covered by Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 (EUDR): it must come from plots with no deforestation after 31/12/2020, backed by a due diligence statement. The regulation applies from 30/12/2026 to large and medium companies and from 30/06/2027 to micro and small companies. Several producing countries, including Ethiopia, are building their own national traceability systems.

    How Sankora supports the coffee sector

    Farmer and plot registry

    Keep your farmer registry and import the GPS points and polygons mapped with your own tools or your service providers' (Excel, KML, KMZ, GeoJSON, GPX). Sankora checks and corrects them automatically: closure, self-intersection, overlap, excluded areas.

    Traceable purchases and lots

    Weighings linked to plots, cash, mobile money, transfer or cheque payments, stocks, lots and shipments. A lot is compliant only if all its weight comes from compliant plots.

    EUDR compliance

    Deforestation analysis for each plot (Global Forest Watch: JRC 2020 forest map, UMD tree cover loss, GLAD and RADD alerts), protected area and classified forest checks, and risk assessment by lot, shipment, contract or supplier, validated by a second person.
